Lab 1 — Module 1: Foundations & Ecosystem Map

Origins & evolution · kill chain · notable incidents · defence builder · attacker-side industry · defender-side industry
Section 1 — Origins & Evolution of Ransomware
Q1 (q1a). What single innovation did Maze introduce in 2019 that made backups alone an insufficient defence? (10 pts)
  • ○ A. Introducing FIDO2-bypassing authentication attacks
  • ○ B. Formalising the affiliate 70/30 revenue split
  • ✓ C. Double extortion: exfiltrating data before encrypting, then threatening public release
  • ○ D. Intermittent encryption to maximise throughput on large file shares
Teaching point: Double extortion — exfiltrating data before encrypting, then threatening public release — meant a clean restore no longer ended the crisis. Victims now face a separate decision about whether to pay to suppress the leak.
Q2 (q1b). Bitcoin enabled ransomware at scale — but also handed defenders a structural lever. What is it? (10 pts)
  • ✓ A. Pseudonymous payment channel — but the permanent public ledger became the tool defenders and law enforcement use to trace and seize funds
  • ○ B. Truly anonymous with no transaction traceability
  • ○ C. It was faster to transfer than wire transfers
  • ○ D. Its price volatility automatically increased ransom value
Teaching point: Bitcoin provided a global, pseudonymous, automatable payment channel — enabling ransomware at scale. But the permanent public ledger is what blockchain analytics firms, defenders, and law enforcement use to trace payments, map the criminal economy, and seize funds.
Section 2 — Kill Chain & Detection-Friendly Behaviours
Q3 (multi-select). Which of the following are high-confidence pre-encryption signals (kill-chain stages 1–6)? Select ALL that apply. (20 pts — 5 correct picks, 0 wrong)
  • ✓ Mass vssadmin shadow copy deletion
  • ✓ Rclone upload to unfamiliar cloud endpoint from a server
  • ✓ ScreenConnect installed on a finance workstation at 02:00
  • ✓ Anomalous Kerberos ticket request surge
  • ✓ bcdedit /set recoveryenabled No
  • ✗ Ransom note dropped in every directory (distractor — this is the extortion stage, not pre-encryption)
  • ✗ High-entropy file I/O across thousands of files simultaneously (distractor — this IS the encryption event, not pre-encryption)
Teaching point: If detection only fires at the encryption event (stage 7) or the ransom note (stage 8), the attacker has already won. Stage 1–6 signals fire hours to days earlier and provide the actionable detection window.
Q4 (q2b). Why is ScreenConnect on a sysadmin laptop "normal" — but on a finance workstation at 02:00 an indicator of compromise? (10 pts)
  • ✓ A. Context determines legitimacy — EDR behavioural analytics detect deviations from baseline, not the tool name itself
  • ○ B. Finance workstations have allow-listing that blocks it
  • ○ C. ScreenConnect is blocked by most EDR platforms by default
  • ○ D. 02:00 is outside business hours so any activity is suspicious
Teaching point: Attackers use legitimate remote-management tools precisely because they evade signature-based detection. Context — user, device, time, baseline behaviour — is what EDR behavioural analytics use to distinguish legitimate admin work from attacker activity.
Section 3 — Notable Incidents & What They Taught Us
Q5 (q3a). What is the defining lesson of Colonial Pipeline? (10 pts)
  • ○ A. Ransomware operators run as structured businesses — pressure on profit margin outlasts infrastructure disruption (Conti / ecosystem lesson)
  • ○ B. Supply-chain compromise via software update proves MSPs are force-multipliers for risk (NotPetya / Kaseya lesson)
  • ✓ C. A single weak credential on a legacy access path — no MFA, reused password — can produce nation-impacting consequences
  • ○ D. English-speaking social engineering bypasses technical controls — help-desk verification is a control surface (MGM / Scattered Spider lesson)
Teaching point: A single VPN account — reused password, no MFA — cut 45% of US East Coast fuel supply and triggered an executive order. The credential hygiene lesson is unambiguous.
Q6 (q3b). The Merck v. ACE American appellate ruling (2023) related to NotPetya was significant for cyber insurance because: (10 pts)
  • ○ A. It established insurers must reimburse all ransom payments regardless of attribution
  • ○ B. It required governments to reimburse cyber-insurance carriers for nation-state losses
  • ✓ C. It found the war exclusion did NOT apply to NotPetya — prompting insurers to rewrite explicit cyber-war exclusion clauses
  • ○ D. It confirmed the war exclusion applied to nation-state cyberattacks, reducing insurer liability
Teaching point: The court found the war exclusion did NOT cover NotPetya — a wiper attributed to Russian GRU. Insurers responded by writing explicit "cyber war" exclusions whose wording remains actively contested.

Section 5 — The Attacker-Side Industry
Q7 (q5a). Which ecosystem role is described? "They convert the noisy work of finding a foothold — credential stuffing, exploiting an unpatched VPN — into a tradable inventory item. Their existence lowers the skill floor for ransomware affiliates." (10 pts)
  • ○ A. Infostealer operator (harvests credentials from individual machines and sells logs — upstream of IABs)
  • ○ B. Affiliate (performs the attack inside the environment — may buy access, but is not the one selling it)
  • ✓ C. Initial Access Broker (IAB)
  • ○ D. RaaS Operator (builds the locker and platform — does not sell initial access)
Teaching point: IABs are arguably the most important specialisation in the modern ecosystem. They commoditise initial access — domain admin at a multinational can fetch $100,000+. Their existence means a skilled lateral-movement specialist no longer needs to be a skilled intruder too.
Q8 (q5b). The "CIS rule" hard-coded into most major ransomware families does what — and what does it reveal about the geopolitical context? (10 pts)
  • ○ A. It requires affiliates to report attack telemetry to Russian intelligence services
  • ○ B. It specifies which encryption algorithm to use for CIS-region targets
  • ○ C. It directs affiliates to prioritise CIS-region targets for highest ransom yield
  • ✓ D. The locker checks system locale and refuses to run on Russian/CIS-language systems — a political instruction that explains Russian government tolerance of the operations
Teaching point: The locker checks Windows locale and keyboard layout and refuses to run on Russian/CIS-language systems. This hard-coded political instruction explains why the Russian government has tolerated these operations — they exclusively target Western enterprises and critical infrastructure.

  • ○ D. The victim and the ransomware operator only
Teaching point: The September 2021 OFAC advisory explicitly named cyber insurers and IR firms (including negotiators) as subjects of sanctions risk. This is why wallet-level blockchain analytics (not just brand-level SDN list check) is now a standard step in any negotiation engagement.

Section 1 — Initial Access & Persistence
Q1. Citrix Bleed (CVE-2023-4966) allowed attackers to bypass MFA. What is the CVE identifier? (10 pts)

Q2. Which MFA method is most susceptible to Adversary-in-the-Middle (AitM) proxy attacks? (10 pts)
  • ✓ A. Push-notification MFA (e.g., Microsoft Authenticator)
  • ○ B. Hardware FIDO2 security key
  • ○ C. Time-based OTP (TOTP)
  • ○ D. Certificate-based smartcard
Teaching point: Push MFA proxied in real-time; FIDO2 keys are phishing-resistant because the challenge is domain-bound.
Q3. Why do attackers prefer legitimate Remote Access Tools (RATs) over custom malware for persistence? (10 pts)
  • ✓ A. Signed binaries blend with admin activity and are often allowlisted in EDR
  • ○ B. Custom malware is too slow to deploy at scale
  • ○ C. RATs provide encrypted C2 channels that custom malware cannot
  • ○ D. Antivirus vendors never update signatures for commercial RATs
Section 2 — Privilege Escalation & Lateral Movement
Q4. What does PowerShell ScriptBlock logging (Event ID 4104) capture that module logging does not? (10 pts)
  • ○ A. The process that launched PowerShell
  • ✓ B. Actual content of commands, including decoded and deobfuscated scripts
  • ○ C. Network connections initiated by the script
  • ○ D. The user account running the process
Teaching point: ScriptBlock logging captures the script AFTER PowerShell decodes it — critical for catching encoded/obfuscated payloads.
Q5. For a Kerberoasting attack to succeed, what must be true? (10 pts)
  • ○ A. The attacker must have domain admin rights
  • ✓ B. A Service Principal Name (SPN) is registered and the account password is weak enough to crack offline
  • ○ C. The Kerberos KDC must be accessible from the internet

Q6. What does a successful DCSync attack achieve? (10 pts)
  • ✓ A. Replicates all AD password hashes by mimicking Domain Controller replication traffic
  • ○ B. Synchronises clocks between domain controllers to bypass Kerberos
  • ○ C. Forces a full AD synchronisation, causing a temporary outage
  • ○ D. Overwrites the SYSVOL share to distribute a malicious logon script
Teaching point: DCSync uses legitimate MS-DRSR protocol; requires Replicating Directory Changes All privilege. Detection: 4662 events on the DC.

Lab 4 — Module 4: Recovery Architecture

Recovery problem statement · backup architecture · immutable storage · IRE & sequencing · restore testing · pre-incident readiness
Section 1 — The Recovery Problem Statement
Q1 (s1q1). According to Mandiant M-Trends, what does median ransomware dwell time mean for your backup infrastructure? (10 pts)
  • ○ A. Attackers rarely find backup systems
  • ○ B. Backup systems are protected by default encryption
  • ○ C. Dwell time only matters for endpoint telemetry
  • ✓ D. Attackers have time to inventory, credential-harvest, and stage destructive actions against backups
Teaching point: Weeks of dwell time gives attackers ample opportunity to enumerate and target backup infrastructure — often before any encryption event is visible to defenders.
Q2 (s1q2). What is the primary time-consuming step in ransomware recovery? (10 pts)
  • ○ A. Restoring bytes from backup tapes
  • ○ B. Rebuilding physical hardware
  • ○ C. Notifying regulators of the incident
  • ✓ D. Validating restored systems are clean — no implants or persistence
Teaching point: The data restore is often relatively fast. The bottleneck is forensic validation that a restored image is truly clean before it is re-introduced to the network.
Section 2 — Backup Architecture (3-2-1+ Model)
Q3 (s2q1). Why is the classic 3-2-1 backup rule insufficient against ransomware? (10 pts)
  • ✓ A. It does not survive an attacker with privileged access who targets backup infrastructure
  • ○ B. It only applies to tape-based backups
  • ○ C. It was designed for cloud environments only
  • ○ D. It requires three copies which is too expensive
Teaching point: 3-2-1 was designed against hardware failure, not adversarial actors. A domain admin can reach all three copies if they share the same identity plane — immutability and identity separation are the missing ingredients.
Q4 (s2q2). What is the most dangerous architectural failure in backup design? (10 pts)
  • ✓ A. Backup management server domain-joined to production AD — a domain admin compromise equals backup compromise
  • ○ B. Using cloud storage for Tier 3
  • ○ C. Running backups during business hours
  • ○ D. Keeping more than 90 days of retention
Teaching point: When the backup server lives in the same AD domain, a domain admin compromise gives the attacker full control over the backup system. This is the most common and consequential single point of failure in backup architecture.
Q5. The critical failure mode for simultaneous destruction of all backup tiers is: (10 pts)
  • ○ A. All tiers stored in the same geographic region
  • ○ B. All tiers using the same backup software vendor
  • ○ C. Insufficient retention period across all tiers
  • ✓ D. All backup tiers sharing the same identity and authentication plane as production
Teaching point: Geographic separation, vendor diversity, and retention windows all matter. But the single most catastrophic failure mode is shared identity — a single domain admin compromise that reaches every tier at once. Identity separation is the architectural property that defeats simultaneous destruction.
Section 3 — Immutable Storage Options
Q6 (s3q1). What is the primary attack vector against cloud object-lock immutability? (10 pts)
  • ✓ A. Account-takeover via cloud IAM compromise or root-account abuse to disable the lock
  • ○ B. Direct encryption of S3 objects
  • ○ C. DDoS against the cloud provider API
  • ○ D. Physical access to the data centre
Teaching point: Immutability protects the objects but not the control plane. An attacker with cloud root credentials can modify or remove retention policies. Cloud IAM hygiene and root-account lockdown are prerequisites for cloud immutability to be meaningful.
Q7 (s3q2). When using vendor-native immutability (Veeam, Rubrik, etc.), what is the critical verification to perform? (10 pts)
  • ✓ A. That immutability cannot be revoked from the same management console used to manage backups
  • ○ B. That the vendor supports multi-cloud environments
  • ○ C. That the vendor has SOC 2 certification
  • ○ D. That backups run during off-peak hours
Teaching point: If an attacker compromises the backup console and immutability can be revoked from that same console, your immutability provides no real protection. The revocation path must be hardened or separated to a different identity boundary.
Section 4 — IRE & Recovery Sequencing
Q8 (s4q1). Why must identity recovery (Active Directory / IdP) happen in Wave 1 before anything else? (10 pts)
  • ○ A. Regulators require AD to be restored first
  • ○ B. AD servers are usually the fastest to restore
  • ○ C. Domain controllers are less likely to be compromised
  • ✓ D. Identity is the recovery substrate — everything else depends on authenticated access to function
Teaching point: Every system you restore — ERP, email, file shares — authenticates against your identity provider. If AD is compromised and you restore other systems against it, those systems immediately inherit the compromise. Identity is the foundation the entire recovery rests on.
Q9 (s4q2). What is the primary purpose of an Isolated Recovery Environment (IRE)? (10 pts)
  • ○ A. To host the incident command post during a crisis
  • ○ B. To run forensic investigations on attacker tooling
  • ○ C. To store backup tapes offline
  • ✓ D. To provide a clean, network-separated environment for restoring and validating systems before re-introducing them to production
Teaching point: The IRE exists to break the cycle of re-infection: restore into isolation, validate clean, then promote to production. Without this step, a restored system introduced to a still-compromised network may be re-infected within hours.
Q10. Before beginning Wave 2 (application recovery), what must be validated first? (10 pts)
  • ○ A. All endpoints have been reimaged
  • ○ B. Payment decision has been made
  • ✓ C. Restored domain controllers are verified clean before being trusted as the identity substrate
  • ○ D. The ransom deadline has passed
Teaching point: Wave 2 (financial, customer-facing, regulated systems) inherits the trust of the identity provider it authenticates against. If Wave 1 identity recovery has not been validated as clean, every Wave 2 system inherits the compromise. Endpoint reimaging, payment status, and deadlines are unrelated to this sequencing constraint.
Section 5 — Restore Testing
Q11 (s5q1). What is the most common gap found when organisations actually try to restore from their primary backups during an incident? (10 pts)
  • ○ A. Backups are too large to restore quickly
  • ○ B. The restore process works exactly as documented
  • ○ C. Cloud storage is always slower than on-prem for restoration
  • ✓ D. Organisations discover they cannot fully restore — due to catalog issues, key escrow gaps, or untested procedures
Teaching point: The most painful discovery during an incident is that backups exist but are not restorable — corrupted catalogs, missing encryption keys, outdated procedures, or agents never validated in a restore scenario.
Q12 (s5q2). Why should restore tests be run in an isolated environment, not in production? (10 pts)
  • ○ A. Regulators require isolated testing
  • ✓ B. A compromised or unstable restored image should not be introduced to the live network until validated clean
  • ○ C. Production environments do not have enough storage
  • ○ D. Production restore tests are too expensive
Teaching point: Restoring directly into production risks re-introducing persistence, implants, or instability from a compromised backup. The IRE provides a safe validation stage before production re-introduction.
Q13 (Multi-Select). Genuine Restore Capability Gaps — select ALL options that would genuinely invalidate restore capability. (20 pts — all 4 correct; 10 pts for 3 of 4)
  • ✓ Backup catalog not accessible without production AD
  • ✓ Immutable retention period shorter than assumed attacker dwell time
  • ✓ Restore procedure never tested — only documented
  • ✓ Backup admin credentials shared with production admin accounts
  • ✗ Using cloud object storage for the immutable tier (distractor — cloud object lock is a valid immutable tier when IAM is hardened)
  • ✗ Running backup jobs during business hours (distractor — scheduling does not invalidate recovery)
Teaching point: Real gaps are architectural and operational: catalog dependency on the compromised identity plane, immutability windows too short to outlast dwell time, untested procedures, and shared admin credentials. Schedule and tier choice are not the failure modes.
